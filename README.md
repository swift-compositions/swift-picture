# swift-picture

Use this package when you describe a frame as a value and hand it to something that draws it: flat shapes with fills and strokes, gradients and images, text, groups under a transform and a clip, a framing that pans and zooms a picture, layers with opacity, blend modes and effects, glows, bloom and rays, additive particles, meshes seen through a camera, through haze and blurred by the camera's travel, and glass: one material, `Picture.Glass`, that is a mesh's body in a scene or a flat pane over the picture with a rounded, lensing rim. Start from `Picture`, a list of `Picture.Element`s. A string set by swift-typesetting's `Typesetting.Text` is drawn here as its glyphs' paths, so that text reaches a renderer as paths. Draw through `Picture.Renderer`, a value holding one closure that draws a picture into a `Raster<Compositing.Premultiplied<Float>>`; it is a `Renderer.Protocol` of swift-renderer and a `Witness.Key` of swift-witnesses, so a renderer is installed and replaced per scope. The `Picture` product fixes what a picture means and gives the exact arithmetic a renderer needs (a colour's pixel, a paint's pixel at a point, the point an effect reads, a sprite's light, where a scene puts a point); the `Picture CPU` product draws with it: `Picture.Renderer.cpu` is a renderer in pure Swift for every platform, the reference for any faster one, and the live, preview and test value of the witness. The `Picture PNG Integration` product turns a drawn raster into a `W3C_PNG.Image` and a parsed one back into a raster, which `Picture.Image` shows; it is empty unless the package's `PNG` trait is on, which is what brings swift-w3c-png into the graph. The `Picture Test Support` product holds `Picture.Reading`, which reads the words a picture shows from its value: shapes whose contours are a font's glyphs at any size, place and turn, gathered into lines along their baselines, through groups, visible layers and kept images, so a test asks what a frame says without drawing it. The `Picture Store` product keeps what was made of drawn pictures in a directory, so a later run reads it back instead of drawing; it is empty unless the `Store` trait is on, which brings swift-file-system, swift-fips-180-4 and swift-rfc-1950. If you only need pixels, use swift-raster; if you only need paths, use swift-geometry.

## Laws

- Laws that begin "A renderer" bind whoever draws a picture; the tests of `Picture CPU` check each of them for `Picture.Renderer.cpu`.
- Picture space is the raster's own. A point (x, y) of `Picture.Space` is the position (x, y) of the raster a picture is drawn into, in pixels, as swift-raster has it: the origin is the outer corner of the first pixel of the first row, x grows along a row, y grows from row to row, and pixel (x, y) covers the square from (x, y) up to (x + 1, y + 1). Rows are shown top first, so on a screen y points down. swift-geometry names things for y up, so its "counterclockwise" and "lower-left" look clockwise and upper-left on screen; winding rules and stroke outlines do not depend on that. Geometry is `Picture.Plane`, which is swift-geometry's `Geometry<Double, Picture.Space>`, so a path is a `Picture.Plane.Path` and a point a `Picture.Plane.Point<2>`; pixels are `Float`.
- A picture never depends on the raster's size: drawn into a larger raster it shows the same things at the same pixels, and what falls outside is not drawn. The one exception is glass near the raster's edge, which reads the nearest pixel kept past the canvas (below).
- `Picture.Color` is a colour as written: sRGB-encoded red, green and blue with straight alpha. Nothing is clamped, and two colours are equal when they are written the same. `linear` puts each of red, green and blue through `IEC_61966.SRGB.TransferFunction.linear(_:)` and leaves alpha alone. `pixel` is that multiplied by alpha, a `Compositing.Premultiplied<Float>`. This is the only way a written colour becomes a pixel, so everything a picture mixes, it mixes in linear light.
- A `Picture` is its elements in order, the first at the back. `appending(_:)` lists this picture's elements and then the other's, and `Picture.empty` is neutral on both sides. Pictures are equal when their elements are equal in order. Every part of a picture is `Sendable` and `Hashable`.
- A `Picture.Shape` is a path with a fill, a stroke or both. A renderer draws the fill first and the stroke on top. The fill covers the points the path contains under the fill's `Winding.Rule`. The stroke covers the points its outline contains under `nonzero`, and `Picture.Shape.Stroke.outline(of:tolerance:)` is that outline: the path's `stroked(_:tolerance:)` of swift-geometry with the stroke's pen, failing with the same `Geometry.Path.Error`. A renderer puts the paint's pixel at each covered point over what is behind, with the share of a pixel's square that is covered as coverage.
- `Picture.Element.fill(_:_:rule:)` is a shape with only a fill, and `stroke(_:_:pen:)` and `stroke(_:_:width:join:cap:)` a shape with only a stroke. `Picture.Transform.translation(x:y:)`, `scale(_:about:)` and `rotation(_:about:)` are swift-matrix's `translation(_:)`, `scale(_:about:)` and `rotation(_:about:)` for a point of picture space; a positive turn moves x toward y, clockwise on a screen. `Picture.Transform(placing:at:turn:scale:)` is the similarity that takes an anchor to a place, in this order: the anchor moved to the origin, scaled, turned, and moved to the place, so p goes to place + scale · R(turn) · (p − anchor).
- `Picture.Paint.pixel(at:)` is the pixel a paint gives at a point of the shape's space. `solid` gives its colour's pixel everywhere.
- `Picture.Gradient.pixel(at:)` reads the stops in the order given. Before the first stop's offset, and for a parameter that is not a number, it is the first stop's pixel; from the last stop's offset on it is the last stop's pixel; between two stops it is the first one's pixel interpolated to the second's by `Compositing.Premultiplied.interpolated(to:at:)`, at the way from the first offset times one over the gap, so colour and alpha move together, premultiplied, in linear light. An offset below the one before it counts as equal to it. Two stops at one offset are a hard edge that takes the later stop at that offset. No stops give transparent. `Picture.Gradient.Ramp(_:)` is the gradient made ready to be read many times: the stops' offsets and their colours as working pixels, taken out of sRGB once, when the stops are set, and kept with them, so `pixel(at:)` and `color(at:)` read it without making it again. Two gradients are equal when their stops are. Its `pixel(at:)` is the gradient's, bit for bit, and the gradient's reads through it, so a renderer makes the ramp once for a shape. `place(of:)` is where a parameter falls as `pixel(at:)` reads it: the stop it goes from, the stop it goes to and the fraction between, one stop as both ends before the first offset, from the last on and for a parameter that is not a number, and nil without stops. `Picture.Gradient.color(at:)` is the colour there as written: the stop itself on a stop and beyond the ends, `Picture.Color(_:)` of `pixel(at:)` between two stops, and `clear` without stops. `Picture.Color(_:)` of a pixel is the way back from `pixel`: colour over alpha through `IEC_61966.SRGB.TransferFunction.encoded(_:)`, nothing clamped, and `clear` for a pixel without alpha.
- `Picture.Color.oklab` is the colour's place in Oklab by swift-color-standard, without alpha and with red, green and blue held to 0…1 first; `Picture.Color.init(_:alpha:)` writes a place in Oklab back in sRGB, each component held to 0…1, so a place outside sRGB comes back as the nearest corner or edge by component. `interpolated(inOklabTo:at:)` is the colour that part of the way along the straight line between the two places, with alpha going the same part of the way. `Picture.Gradient.oklab(steps:)` gives a gradient that goes from stop to stop through Oklab: each stretch between two stops becomes `steps` equal stretches whose new stops are those mixes. `pixel(at:)` still reads each short stretch in linear light, so the gradient meets Oklab at its stops and comes closer between them with more steps. A hard edge stays one, and fewer than two steps or fewer than two stops change nothing. `Picture.Gradient(evenly:)` puts the i-th of n colours at i / (n − 1); one colour is one stop at 0 and none give no stops.
- `Picture.Color(hex:alpha:)` reads `0xRRGGBB` through `IEC_61966.SRGB(r255:g255:b255:)`, each component its byte over 255, and ignores higher bits; the `String` form reads `#RGB` or `#RRGGBB` through `IEC_61966.SRGB(hex:)` and is nil for anything else. `with(alpha:)` replaces alpha, and `faded(by:)` multiplies it by an opacity, unclamped. `scaled(by:)` multiplies the colour's light: red, green and blue in linear light times the factor, written back through `encoded(_:)`, alpha kept and nothing clamped, so its pixel is this one's with the colour times the factor and a factor above 1 is light brighter than white. `interpolated(inSRGBTo:at:)` goes straight between the colours as written, `self + (other − self) × share` for each component and alpha, the share not held: a mix of sRGB-encoded values, where `interpolated(inOklabTo:at:)` mixes perceptually and a gradient mixes in linear light.
- `Picture.Paint.Linear.parameter(at:)` is how far along the line from `start` to `end` the point's foot lies, 0 at `start` and 1 at `end`, and beyond both; a line with no length gives 0 everywhere. `Picture.Paint.Radial.parameter(at:)` is the distance from the centre in radii; a radius that is not positive gives 0 everywhere. The paint's pixel is the gradient's pixel at that parameter, so a gradient holds its end colours outside 0 to 1.
- A falloff is light that falls as the inverse square of the distance past a core and is brought to nothing at its radius. `Picture.Gradient.falloff(at:core:)` is, for a distance d in radii, `(1 − d²)³ / (1 + (d / core)²)`: 1 at the centre and for every d ≤ 0, 0 from 1 on and for a distance that is not a number, never growing, and half the window at the core. The window meets 0 with no slope and no bend, so that the light's sRGB encoding meets 0 with no slope either and a falloff shows no edge. A core that is not positive leaves the window alone. `Picture.Gradient.falloff(_:core:stops:)` is the colour, the same law as `falloff(at:core:)` painted, faded by the falloff at `stops` offsets, at least two, the i-th of n at (i / (n − 1))², read straight between them in linear light: with the default 32 stops it paints the falloff to within 0.0015 of the colour at the default core of 0.25, and within 0.003 for any core from 0.1. `Picture.Paint.falloff(_:center:radius:core:)` is that gradient as a radial paint, and `Picture.Element.falloff(_:center:radius:core:)` fills the square around its circle with it. A falloff over what is behind covers it as far as the falloff goes; in a layer under `lighter` it adds its light.
- A `Picture.Image` shows its `content`, `.pixels` of a raster of working pixels or `.drawing` of a `Picture.Image.Source` a renderer has still to draw (below), so a drawing never carries pixels. With the identity `placement` the raster's first pixel's corner is at the origin and one pixel is one unit. `placement` is a projective map of the plane, a `Matrix<3, 3, Double>` of swift-matrix; an affine one (`init(_:placement:sampling:edge:)` takes a `Picture.Transform` and lifts it) moves the image as a group's transform moves its content, and any other shows it in perspective. `pixel(at:)` samples the raster at the point the inverse placement gives, by `projected(_:)` of swift-matrix, and for an affine placement by the affine inverse, with the image's own sampling and edge, mixing by `interpolated(to:at:)`. By default sampling is bilinear and outside the raster is transparent. A placement that cannot be inverted squashes the image to no area and it paints nothing; so does a point that is not finite. A raster with no pixels paints its edge's constant, or nothing. `Picture.Image.Sampler(_:)` is the image with its placement inverted once; its `pixel(at:)` is the image's, bit for bit, and the image's reads through it, so a renderer inverts the placement once for a shape.
- A `Picture.Group` holds a picture, a transform and an optional clip. A renderer draws the content's point p at `transform.applied(to: p)`, so a group inside a group draws at `outer.multiplied(by: inner)`. The clip is in the content's space and moves with it. A renderer draws a group's elements straight onto what is behind, each limited by the clip: where the clip covers a share c of a pixel, the pixel ends as what was there plus c times the change the element would have made. Clips inside clips multiply. A group with the identity and no clip draws exactly as its elements would in its place.
- A `Picture.Framing` is what part of a picture the frame shows and how large, a similarity of the plane that pans and zooms (an eye that looks into a scene is a `Picture.Scene.Camera`): a point of the picture, `focus`, shown at a point of the frame, `center`, `scale` times larger than drawn, so a point p is shown at `center + scale × (p − focus)`, which is `place(of:)` and `transform`; calling it on a picture gives a group under that transform. `interpolated(to:at:)` takes the scale by equal ratios, `scale · exp(share · L)` for L the logarithm of the ratio of the scales, and the focus `expm1(−share · L) / expm1(−L)` of the way (the share itself when L is 0), so between framings of one centre the one point of the picture both show at the same place stays there; the centre always goes straight, and a scale that is not positive gives this framing below a share of 1 and the other from 1 on. `scaled(by:about:)` multiplies the scale and keeps the point's place on the frame. `smear(since:)` is the `zoom` centred on the frame point both framings put one picture point at, with amount `1 − 1 / ratio` for the ratio of the scales, nil for equal scales or a scale that is not positive. `exposure(_:since:shutter:_:)` is the picture shown through a shutter open for the last `shutter` of the way from an earlier framing, which opened at `earlier.interpolated(to: self, at: 1 − shutter)`, blurred as a `Picture.Exposure` says: `samples(n)` is `Picture.exposure(samples:_:)` of the picture under the framings between, exact for every travel; `bands(n)` is the view at the opening in a layer under `smear(since:)` of it, one band whatever n since the picture is flat. The smear is a zoom, so it shows a change of scale; a framing that only moves sideways leaves no smear, and `samples` is the way to blur that. A shutter that is not positive, a framing that did not move, no samples and no bands give the plain view.
- A `Picture.Layer` holds a picture, effects, an opacity, a blend mode and an operator. A renderer draws the content on a transparent sheet of its own, applies the effects in order, and then puts each pixel of the sheet onto the pixel behind it with `Picture.Layer.composited(_:over:)`, which is `composited(over:operator:blend:coverage:)` of the W3C compositing target with the opacity as coverage. By default there are no effects, the opacity is 1, the blend mode is `normal` and the operator is `sourceOver`. Under `lighter` the colours add without a limit and alpha is held at 1, so light added to an opaque pixel leaves it opaque. Shapes inside a layer see only the sheet, never what is behind the layer. Glass is the one element that reads what is behind it, and inside a layer it reads the picture as it stands beneath the layer: the sheet so far put with `sourceOver` onto what is beneath the layer, itself read so through every layer around it, as if each ended there at full strength and without its effects. What the glass leaves on the sheet is then composited with the layer's opacity, blend mode and operator, so a pane or a body at opacity p shows what it stands over mixed p of the way to the glass's result, at 0 no glass and at 1 the glass as it is outside any layer, wherever in groups and layers it stands.
- A `Picture.Effect` reads the sheet as a picture on the whole plane, transparent where nothing was drawn, in the units of the layer's own space; a renderer must not let the edge of its raster show in the result. Every effect but `blur` names the points it reads, and a renderer reads the sheet there.
- `blur` is a convolution with a Gaussian of the given standard deviation along both axes; a deviation that is not positive changes nothing. `Picture.Effect.Blur.followed(by:)` is two blurs as one: the deviation is the root of the sum of the squares.
- `zoom` is a radial blur: a renderer gives each point the mean of the sheet at `source(of:at:)` for fractions from 0 to 1. That point runs along the line toward the centre, from the point itself at fraction 0 to `amount` of the way to the centre at fraction 1. The centre reads itself, and an amount of 0 changes nothing.
- `chromatic` moves red, green and blue apart: a renderer gives each point `pixel(red:green:blue:)` of the sheet's pixels at the point minus the red offset, minus the green offset and minus the blue offset. That pixel takes each channel from its own pixel and the largest of the three alphas, so three equal pixels give that pixel and no channel exceeds the alpha.
- `pixelation` fills every cell of a grid with what is at its centre: `source(of:)` is the centre of the cell holding the point, where a cell holds its lower edges and not its upper ones and one cell's corner is at `origin`. It reads its own result unchanged. A cell with no width or height reads the point itself.
- `kaleidoscope` cuts the plane around `center` into `segments` equal sectors, the first starting at the direction `rotation`, each sector being two mirror-image wedges. `source(of:)` is the point's mirror image in the first wedge, at the same distance from the centre, so a point in the first wedge reads itself and the result reads itself. With no segments, and at the centre, a point reads itself. Angles come from the scalar's own `atan2`, `sin` and `cos`, so the result is as repeatable across platforms as those are. The mirrors are the n = `segments` lines through the centre at `rotation` plus whole multiples of π / n; they cut the plane into 2n wedges, `wedge(of:)` counting from the first toward growing angle, the even ones the first turned and the odd ones its mirror image. So the result is invariant under a turn by 2π / n and under the reflection in each mirror, for odd n as for even. The number of wedges is even for every n, so every other wedge alternates all the way round; for even n each wedge faces one of its own parity across the centre and the result is also invariant under a half turn, for odd n each faces one of the other parity and it is not. `distance(of:)` is the distance to the nearest half-line of a mirror, and `holds(_:)` whether the first wedge, edges and centre included, holds a point. With `spokes`, the odd wedges are covered by that colour: `spoke(at:footprint:)` is the share of a stretch `footprint` wide around the point's signed distance into them that lies in them (from 0 to 1, a half on an edge, 1 or 0 for a footprint that is not positive), and the pixel is interpolated to the spokes' pixel by it, so a clear colour masks them; what stays is the first wedge turned n times, invariant under a turn by 2π / n, and each mirror's reflection exchanges spokes and the rest. With a `seam`, `seam(at:footprint:)` is the overlap of that stretch around the distance to the nearest mirror with the seam's `width` around the mirror, over the footprint, so a seam thinner than the footprint is fainter, not wider, and the seam's pixel faded by it is put over. `pixel(_:at:footprint:)` is both in that order, the read pixel unchanged without them.
- `displacement` reads each point from `source(of:)`, the point plus `offset(at:)`. The offset's x is `amplitude` times `Noise<3, Double>` of swift-noise with the seed, at (x × `frequency`, y × `frequency`, `phase`); its y is the same with the seed's second word one higher. Each is at most `amplitude` in size, zero where all three noise coordinates are whole numbers, and bit-identical on every platform, as that noise is. `offset(at:caches:)` and `source(of:caches:)` give the same offsets bit for bit through a `Caches` value, one for each of the two fields, which keeps each field's last lattice cell; one `Caches` per thread.
- `grain` cuts the plane into square cells `size` wide, one cell's corner at the origin. `Picture.Effect.Grain.cell(at:)` is the cell that holds a point, and `share(of:)` the cell's share of `amount`, from −1 to 1: the mean of two even draws of `Threefry.block` with the seed as key and the cell's column and row as counter, so it is bit-identical on every platform. `pixel(_:share:)` moves red, green and blue alike by `amount` times the share times the pixel's alpha, held between 0 and the larger of the component and the alpha, and leaves alpha alone; `share(at:)` and `pixel(_:at:)` are the same for the cell that holds a point. A renderer changes each pixel of the sheet by the grain at the pixel's centre taken back into the layer's space, and may draw a cell's share once for all its pixels.
- `swirl` turns everything within `radius` of `center` about it: `turn(at:)` is `angle`, a `Radian<Double>`, times the square of what is left of the radius as a share of it, so the turn falls to nothing at the radius with no kink, and `source(of:)` is the point turned back about the centre by the turn at its distance.
- `threshold` keeps of each pixel only its light above a level. A pixel's brightness b is the largest of its red, green and blue as a working pixel, so a saturated colour counts as fully as white of the same strength. `Picture.Effect.Threshold.kept(_:)` is 0 up to `level`, `(b − level)² / (2 · knee)` over the knee and `b − level − knee / 2` from `level + knee` on: continuous with its slope, never falling, and a straight cut at the level for a knee that is not positive. `pixel(_:)` multiplies colour and alpha alike by the kept light over b, at most 1, so the hue stays and the pixel never grows; a pixel without brightness gives transparent. A renderer changes each pixel of the sheet by it.
- `Picture.glow(deviation:strength:)` and `Picture.backdrop(_:clip:)` add no element kinds; they are compositions. A glow is the picture followed by a layer of the same picture, blurred, with `strength` as opacity and `lighter` as operator. A glow of strength 0 is the picture itself. A glow `sparing:` a mask, a picture in the same space, holds in that layer a layer of the blurred picture and then the mask in a layer under `destinationOut`, so the light keeps one minus the mask's alpha before it is added: where the mask is opaque the picture is drawn as it is, as a ring's glow that leaves its middle dark, and an empty mask is no mask. A backdrop pane is the picture followed by a group with the clip, holding a layer of the same picture under the effects with `copy` as operator: inside the clip the picture is replaced by itself blurred or bent.
- `Picture.bloom(in:threshold:deviation:octaves:strength:scale:resolution:)` and `Picture.rays(from:in:threshold:length:streaks:strength:scale:resolution:)` add no element kinds either. Each keeps the picture as one image of the rectangle at `scale`, `Picture.Image(_:in:scale:)`, and is that image filled over the rectangle, followed by a layer under `lighter` with `strength` as opacity that holds the rectangle filled with a second image, kept at `scale` times `resolution`: the mean, each in a layer under `lighter`, of the first image under `threshold` and then, for bloom, a blur whose deviation halves from `deviation` for each of the `octaves`, and for rays, the k-th of n `streaks` a `zoom` toward the point of amount `length × k / n`. So the picture is drawn once, the light is added and never darkens a pixel, and a picture with nothing above the threshold gives exactly the kept picture, as do no octaves, no streaks, a length of 0 and a strength of 0. Bloom adds `strength` times the light the threshold keeps, with a sharp core and a long tail; rays streak a bright part at a distance r from the point out to r / (1 − length), fading as they go in n steps. Light that would spread past the rectangle is not drawn. The defaults (a threshold at 0.5 with a knee of 0.5, octaves of 24, 12 and 6 for bloom, four streaks up to 0.5 for rays, a strength of 1, half resolution) are made for a frame in pixels of picture space.
- `Picture.mean(of:)` and `Picture.exposure(samples:_:)` add no element kinds either. The mean of several pictures is one layer that holds a layer for each of them, with one part in their number as opacity and `lighter` as operator, so each is drawn on a sheet of its own and the sheets add up to their mean, colour and alpha alike, up to rounding; no pictures give the empty picture and one picture gives itself. An exposure is motion blur: the mean of a picture that changes with a fraction from 0 to 1, taken at the midpoints of `samples` equal parts of that stretch, `(i + ½) / samples`, in order. What moves is smeared along its way and what stands still is drawn as it is. It stops at the first sample that fails. A mean of n pictures costs n sheets and n composites, so about n times the picture.
- `Picture.Image(_:in:scale:samples:sampling:edge:)` keeps the part of a picture inside a rectangle as an image, and leaves the drawing to the renderer: the image holds no pixels, its content is `.drawing` of a `Picture.Image.Source` whose drawing is a `Picture.Image.Part` (the picture, the rectangle, the scale and the samples), and its placement is already the one the drawn raster will have. Making it, comparing it and hashing it draw nothing. A renderer that draws a picture holding such an image draws the source first, as `Picture.Image.Part.raster(through:)` says: the picture once, moved and scaled so that the rectangle's first corner is the raster's and `scale` pixels are one unit, into a transparent raster of the rectangle's size in pixels, each side rounded up. With more than one `samples` it is drawn that many times as fine along each side and each pixel is the mean of its square, by `Raster.resampled` with the box filter. The placement puts the raster back where the part stood, so the image painted in a shape shows the picture there, at the raster's resolution, and can be moved, repeated and drawn again for the price of an image: this is how a picture is nested many times in one frame, or in every sample of an exposure, without being drawn each time. Any value that is described cheaply and drawn dearly can be a source: a `Picture.Image.Drawing` is `Sendable` and `Hashable`, names the `pictures` it draws through the renderer it is given (a part names its picture; none by default), and draws its raster by `raster(through:)`; `Picture.Image(_:placement:sampling:edge:)` of a drawing is an image that names it, as swift-particles names its light. A source keeps its drawing's type out of its own, so one picture holds drawings of many types: `drawing(as:)` gives the drawing back as a type, nil for another, and `pictures` are the ones it names. A `Picture.Image.Source` is hashed once, when it is made, from its drawing's type and value, never from pixels, and every later hash combines that one number, so a renderer looks a source up for the price of a number however large its drawing, and sources whose hashes differ are told apart without comparing their drawings; equal hashes are confirmed by comparing the drawings. An image that holds pixels a caller gave it hashes them once, the first time it is hashed or compared, and keeps that number for every copy, so comparing two copies costs nothing and two images of different pixels are told apart by the number. The picture says what to keep and the renderer decides how: equal sources are drawn once, and a renderer may keep what it drew for the next picture that names an equal source, so a part that stays the same from frame to frame is drawn once for all of them. A rectangle with no area, a scale that is not positive and finite, and a raster with a side of 2³¹ fine pixels or more fail with `.unsupported`; a renderer's failure is the image's. A drawing that gives no raster for a reason of its own, such as pixels read from elsewhere that did not arrive, fails with `.drawing` of a `Picture.Image.Failure`, which holds its own error, any `Error` that is `Hashable`, with its type: `error` is that error and `error(as:)` gives it back as its type, nil for another. That failure reaches whoever drew unchanged, through `raster(through:)`, through `drawn(through:)` and through a renderer that draws a picture holding the image, however deep; a renderer keeps nothing for a drawing that failed, so an equal drawing asked for later is drawn again, and if it succeeds then, it is shown. A test holds this for each of those paths. Until a renderer has drawn it, `pixel(at:)` of such an image paints nothing. `Picture.Element.image(_:in:scale:samples:sampling:edge:filling:)` is an area, the rectangle by default, filled with such an image, the picture kept and put back where it stood; bloom and rays keep their pictures by it. A wider area shows the image's `edge` beyond the rectangle: mirrored, repeated or drawn out. `image(_:in:scale:samples:sampling:edge:onto:)` fits the kept part onto four corners in perspective: the quadrilateral filled with the image under `Matrix<3, 3, Double>(fitting:onto:)` from the rectangle's corners, (llx, lly), (urx, lly), (urx, ury), (llx, ury), onto them, read perspective-correct at every pixel (a test holds that the rectangle's middle lands where the corners' diagonals cross); corners three of which lie on one line fail with `Matrix.Error.singular`. `drawn(through:)` is the image whose content is `.pixels`, and `Picture.Image(_:in:scale:samples:sampling:edge:through:)` is both steps at once, for a caller that needs the pixels itself.
- `Picture.Sprites` are discs of light. `Picture.Sprites.Profile.coverage(at:)` is the share of a sprite's light at a distance from its centre given in radii: `hard` is 1 inside the radius, `soft` is (1 − d²)², and both are 0 from the radius on. `light(at:)` is the sum over the sprites of each one's pixel times its coverage there. It is a sum, so the order of the sprites does not matter up to rounding, and it can exceed 1. A renderer adds that light to what is behind with `lighter`. A sprite with no radius gives no light.
- A `Picture.Scene` draws meshes through a camera into a rectangle of the picture. `Picture.Scene.Camera.clip(_:in:)` is projection times view times model, applied to (x, y, z, 1), with `Matrix(projection)` of swift-matrix. `normalized(_:in:)` divides by the fourth clip coordinate and is nil unless that is positive, so a point in the plane of the eye or behind it has no place. `Picture.Scene.position(of:in:)` maps normalised x from −1…1 onto the viewport's x range and normalised y from 1…−1 onto its y range, by `Projection.Viewport.point(_:)` of swift-projection for a viewport whose y grows downward (a viewport with no area puts every point at its first corner), so up in eye space is toward smaller y, the top of the screen; its depth is the normalised z, lower for nearer points. `Camera.map(of:)` is projection times view times model, and `Camera.clip(_:through:)`, `Camera.normalized(_:through:)` and `Scene.position(of:through:)` are the same three from that product, bit for bit, so a renderer makes the product once per model.
- `Picture.Scene.Camera.distance(of:in:)` is how far ahead of the eye a point lies, minus the z of view times model applied to (x, y, z, 1), negative behind the eye; `distance(of:through:)` is the same from `eye(of:)` made once. `distance(at:)` is the distance of what the camera draws at a normalised depth, minus the z of `Projection.eye(_:)`. `sight(of:in:viewport:)` is a `Sight`: the `Picture.Scene.Position` the scene gives the point, bit for bit (`Picture.Scene.Position(_:in:)` maps normalised coordinates into a viewport for both), its distance, and `ahead`, whether its normalised depth is at least the lower end of the depth range, which is when it lies at or beyond the near plane; nil where the position is. `Picture.Scene.sight(of:in:)` is the camera's through the scene's viewport. `Camera.point(under:distance:viewport:)` is the way back, by `Projection.Viewport.normalized(_:)`, `Projection.eye(_:distance:)` and the inverse of the view as a `Matrix.Rigid`: the point of the world the camera shows at a point of the viewport at a distance ahead of the eye, whose sight is that point and that distance up to rounding (a test holds it for a perspective and an orthographic lens under turned views); nil for a viewport with no area, a point the lens does not take back, or a view that is not a rotation and a move to within 10⁻⁶. `placement(at:distance:viewport:)` is the translation to it, which stands a model's origin there, and `Picture.Scene.point(under:distance:)` and `placement(at:distance:)` are both through the scene's viewport.
- `Picture.Scene.Haze` is a colour every surface of the scene tends toward with its distance ahead of the eye, by the exponential law of fog: `share(at:)` is `1 − exp(−density × distance)`, 0 at the eye and for a distance or density that is not positive, never falling, tending to 1; `pixel(_:at:)` is the surface's pixel interpolated to the haze's by that share, colour and alpha together, so at no distance it is the surface. A scene with a haze draws each sample of every flat, wireframe and points model so, at the distance of the surface there; glass shows the hazed picture behind it and its own tint and light are not hazed, and where no model is, nothing is drawn, so a caller paints the background in the haze's colour.
- `Picture.Scene.Camera.interpolated(to:at:)` is an eye moving and turning evenly between two cameras: the views' inverses, the eyes' own motions, are mixed by `Matrix.Rigid.interpolated(to:at:)` of swift-matrix and inverted back, so the place goes straight and the turn about one axis the shorter way at an even angular rate, and every view between is rigid; the lens is `Projection.interpolated(to:at:)` of swift-projection, whose bounds go straight for lenses of one kind and depth, else it is this lens below a share of 1 and the other's from 1 on. `Picture.Scene.Camera.Pose.interpolated(to:at:)` is the same mix of two poses, exact at 0 and 1, a pose whose rotation is not one mixed entry by entry. A view whose upper-left 3 × 3 is not a rotation to within 10⁻⁶ or whose last row is not (0, 0, 0, 1) is mixed entry by entry. A share of 0 gives this camera, 1 the other, and equal cameras give the camera.
- `Picture.Scene.exposure(since:shutter:_:)` of an earlier scene is the scene blurred by how its camera and its models moved: the earlier scene gives the earlier camera and the earlier transform of each model, paired by place in the list (a model past the end of the earlier list stood still), and each transform goes between as a rigid motion by `Matrix.Rigid.interpolated(to:at:)` where both are one to within 10⁻⁶, its origin straight and its turn even, and entry by entry where either is not; `smear(since:)` of an earlier scene sees each vertex then through the earlier camera and the earlier transform.
- The shutter is open for the last `shutter` of the way from the earlier scene, so it opened at the scene that share of the way, its camera `earlier.camera.interpolated(to: camera, at: 1 − shutter)`: a `Picture` whose motion blur follows the `Picture.Exposure`. A shutter that is not positive, a scene that did not move, and no samples or no bands give `[.scene(self)]`, so a still camera's blur is the unblurred scene. An earlier camera alone is an earlier scene with that camera. `samples(n)` is `Picture.exposure(samples:_:)` of the scene interpolated from the opening to this one at each fraction: exact for every motion, moving and turning alike, and n scenes and n composites. `bands(n)` cuts the models into n bands by the distance of each one's mean vertex, evenly in one over that distance between the farthest and the nearest model (a forward travel smears a point by about its travel over its distance), with a model whose mean vertex is not ahead of the eye in the nearest; it draws them from far to near, each a scene of its models in their order seen by the opening camera, in a layer under `smear(since:)` of the band. Depth is compared within a band and not across bands, so bands that interpenetrate are drawn in band order. `Picture.Scene.smear(since:)` of an earlier scene is the zoom that carries, by least squares over every vertex both scenes see, the frame point where this camera sees it to where the earlier one saw it: a zoom is a scale about a centre, so it is exact for a travel along the line of sight in each band's own depth, and close for a turn, which moves the frame nearly evenly; a shift alone is a zoom of amount 10⁻⁶ about a centre far along it, and fewer than two such vertices or none moving by 10⁻⁶ give nil. Each band is drawn once and smeared once, so it costs about a scene and a zoom over the band's box, and a zoom costs about L / 8 + 8 reads a pixel for a smear L pixels long. So `samples` is exact and, for a scene that draws faster than a zoom of its area, also the cheaper; it shows n distinct copies where a point travels more than about n pixels. `bands` streaks smoothly at any length, but a band's points share one zoom, so an object deep along the line of sight is smeared as its band's mean (the near edge of a near box short by about the share its depth spans), and it is the cheaper only where one scene costs more than a zoom of the frame, as glass with a body does.
- `Picture.Scene.Camera.direction(_:in:)` is a direction of a model in eye space at unit length: view times model applied to (x, y, z, 0), so no translation moves it. It is the zero vector when that has no length. It is right for normals as long as view and model do not scale unevenly. `eye(of:)` is view times model and `direction(_:through:)` the same direction from that product, bit for bit, so a renderer makes the product once per model.
- A `Picture.Scene.Camera.Pose` is where an eye stands, `place`, and its `rotation` from the world's axes to the eye's, whose rows are the eye's right, `up` and backward directions, so `direction`, where it looks, is minus the last row; `view` is the rotation after a move by minus the place, and `Pose(_:)` of a view takes it apart, nil unless it is a rotation and a move to within 10⁻⁶. A camera's `pose` is that of its view, so a caller asks a camera where its eye is. `Pose(at:yaw:pitch:roll:)`, `Pose(around:distance:yaw:pitch:roll:)` and `Pose(at:looking:up:)` are the three ways below to stand an eye, and `Camera(projection:pose:)` is a lens with a pose's view.
- `Picture.Scene.Camera(fieldOfView:aspect:near:far:depth:view:)` is `Projection.perspective` with the same arguments and its failure, and `Camera(focal:viewport:near:far:depth:view:)` that lens for the field of view `Projection.field(focal:height:)` gives a focal length in picture units and the viewport's height, at the viewport's aspect, so a unit one unit ahead is drawn `focal` picture units long; each also takes a `pose:` in place of the view. `Picture.Scene.Camera(projection:at:yaw:pitch:roll:)` stands the eye at a place, turned first by `yaw` about the world's y, then `pitch` about its own x, then `roll` about its line of sight, each by the right-hand rule, so positive angles look left, look up and bank left; its view is `rotation(aboutZ: −roll) · rotation(aboutX: −pitch) · rotation(aboutY: −yaw) · translation(−place)` of swift-matrix. `Camera(projection:around:distance:yaw:pitch:roll:)` is that camera standing `distance` back along its own line of sight from a target, at target + distance · (sin yaw · cos pitch, −sin pitch, cos yaw · cos pitch), so it orbits the target and sees it at the middle of a centred lens. `Camera(projection:at:looking:up:)` takes its view from `Matrix.looking(at:from:up:)` and fails as that does. `Camera.perspective(fieldOfView:aspect:near:far:depth:view:)`, `perspective(focal:viewport:near:far:depth:view:)`, `perspective(distance:scale:viewport:near:far:depth:)` and `looking(at:from:up:projection:)` are those initializers for numbers written in source: what the initializer would throw traps. `Camera(distance:scale:viewport:near:far:depth:)` stands at (0, 0, distance) looking along −z through a centred perspective of the viewport's aspect whose field of view is `Projection.field(focal: scale · distance, height:)` of the viewport's height: the plane z = 0 is drawn `scale` picture units to a unit, the origin in the viewport's middle; it is the focal camera of focal length `scale · distance`.
- `Picture.Scene.Camera.radius(of:at:in:viewport:)` is how large the camera draws a ball, `Projection.radius(of:at:in:)` of swift-projection of the centre in eye space: half the width of its silhouette across the line from the middle of the view, in picture units. Through a perspective lens that is the cone of sight lines grazing the ball, not the radius over the distance: for a centre d from the eye and θ off the line of sight, sin α = radius / d and f the picture units a unit one unit ahead is drawn along y, it is f · sin α / √(cos² θ − sin² α), f · tan α on the line of sight, where the silhouette is a circle, and off it the half width across an ellipse drawn out along that line. Through an orthographic lens it is the radius times the vertical scale. It is nil for a ball that holds or reaches the plane of the eye. A test holds it to within half a pixel of a drawn ball, on the line of sight and off it, where the naive radius over the distance is short by three.
- A renderer draws a scene straight onto what is behind, model after model. Where two things of one scene fall on the same point, the one with the lower depth shows; depth is not compared across scenes. What lies in front of the near plane or beyond the far plane is not drawn, and a face or edge that crosses the near plane is cut there. Both sides of a face are drawn. `flat` fills every face with its colour. `wireframe` draws every distinct edge once, as a line `width` picture units wide whatever its distance. `points` draws every vertex once, as a disc of `radius` picture units whatever its distance. `shaded` takes at each point the `Picture.Scene.Material.Shade`'s `pixel(facing:)` of the surface's normal in eye space there: its `ramp` read at `light(facing:)`, Lambert's cosine to the lamp's `direction`, held at 0 or more, plus `sheen` times (1 − the normal's z)^`sharpness`, the edge-on term a glass light's `sheen` is, a normal facing away turned to face the eye first. `cosine(facing:)` and `sheen(facing:)` are the two terms apart and `light(facing:)` their sum, bit for bit, and `pixel(at:)` the ramp at any light, so a point or a sprite with a normal of its own is lit by the same law. So 0 is the dark side, 1 faces the lamp and the sheen reads past 1, and on a ball the brightest point faces the lamp, which a test holds. The normal is that of the vertices, so a welded mesh is shaded smooth and one whose faces have vertices of their own (`Mesh(_:)` of polygons) flat.
- `glass` is drawn after every other model of its scene and shows only its nearest surface. Where that surface is not hidden, the pixel becomes the glass's pixel (below) over the picture as it stood before any glass, read at the point plus `offset(for:)` of the surface's unit normal in eye space there. For a skin, `.skin(bend:)`, the offset is `bend` times the normal's x, and minus `bend` times its y, so a surface facing the eye reads straight through. The pixel is the tint over what was read; the default tint is clear and changes nothing. `Picture.Glass.Shading(_:surroundings:)` is the glass with its tint and its light's colour taken into working pixels once, and its `pixel(over:through:leaving:beyond:mirroring:by:facing:outline:tilts:)` is the one law of a glass's pixel, every part after `over` optional: without a length the tint lies over what was read, as on a skin; without a normal no light is added.
- Every length of a glass is in units of the picture it is drawn in, the space of the pane or scene before the transforms of the groups around it: its frost, its light's line, its shadows, a pane's thickness and rim, and the `distance` of a pane or of a scene's glass model. So one glass value is one substance on a flat circle, a dome, a sphere and a torus of the same size in the picture, and at any scale of a scene's own units.
- `Picture.Glass` is one material wherever it stands: the `glass` of a scene's model and the glass of a `Picture.Glass.Pane`, with one set of parameters and one set of laws, so a flat bubble and a torus of the same value show the same substance. Each reads the picture behind in the same order: bent by the surface (`offset(for:)` for a skin, Snell's law for a body, at every wavelength the spectrum asks for), frosted, made brighter and more vivid by `gain` and `saturation` (`saturated(_:by:gain:)`: each of red, green and blue becomes gain × (luminance + (component − luminance) × saturation), held to 0 up to alpha, luminance being `Color.luminance(red:green:blue:)` of swift-color-standard, 0.2126 red, 0.7152 green and 0.0722 blue in linear light), adapted, under the tint, and the light added last.
- `frost` is the deviation of a Gaussian over the picture behind, in units of the picture the glass is drawn in; 0 is clear. It thins where the surface turns away: `frosting(facing:)` is the square root of the normal's z, and what the glass reads is the clear picture mixed toward the frosted one by that share, so a level middle is frosted fully and a rim seen edge-on shows the picture lensed and clear, as a polished edge does. `adaptation`, a `Picture.Glass.Adaptation`, moves the lightness of what the glass reads toward a colour's by `strength` times the difference of the two, keeping its hue and chroma: in Oklab (`Color.Oklab.coordinates(red:green:blue:)` of swift-color-standard of the colour over alpha held to 0…1) L moves and a and b stay, and `Color.Oklab.linear(l:a:b:)` takes it back, each component held to 0…1. Toward white a dark backdrop is lifted most and keeps its colour, and one as light as white is left alone, so black labels stay legible; toward black the reverse; none changes nothing.
- `Picture.Glass.Light` adds three things, held together to 0…1, in the light's colour with `lighter`: a highlight where the surface faces halfway between the light and the eye (the cosine to that direction raised to `gloss`, times `highlight`); a line along the glass's outline as drawn (`line(at:across:)`: `line` times a profile that is 1 from the outline to `width` in, falls with no slope at either end to 0 at two and a half widths, and is 0 outside); and a sheen where the surface is edge-on to the eye (`sheen` times (1 − the normal's z)^`sharpness`). Line and sheen take the side's share: for the cosine a between the outline's or the normal's direction across the view and the light's, ((1 + a) / 2)^f + `counter` × ((1 − a) / 2)^f for f the `focus`, so 1 on the side toward the light, `counter` on the side away, and (1 + `counter`) / 2^f across; 1 when either has no direction across the view. A `Picture.Glass.Outline` is where a point lies from the outline: how far in and the `outward` direction across the view. `reflection(for:tilt:)` is the highlight and sheen at the normal leaned away from the eye by `tilt` times its own angle, at most a right angle; the pixel law gives each of red, green and blue its own tilt, its reflection at the normal leaned by it and its line at `inward` times it, so with dispersion the light parts into colours as the light bent through the glass does, and without it every colour takes the same share.
- Dispersion is spectral. The laws are swift-refraction's: `Picture.Glass.Body.medium` is a `Refraction.Cauchy`, an index at the d line and an Abbe number V = (n_d − 1) / (n_F − n_C), by default 45, between crown glass, about 60, and dense flint, about 30, and infinite for no dispersion; the body's `index(at:)` is the medium's Cauchy law, never below 1, and `index` is its own index at the d line, never below 1. `Picture.Glass.Spectrum(count:)` cuts 400…700 nm into `count` equal parts and samples their midpoints, weighting each wavelength by `response(at:)`, the CIE 1931 colour matching functions of swift-iso-11664 in Wyman, Sloan and Shirley's multi-lobe fit taken into linear sRGB and held at 0 or more, each colour's weights divided by their sum, as `Color.Spectrum(sampling:count:observer:)` of swift-color-standard samples them: so a flat white is gathered back to white (`gathered(_:)`), and a count of one is the d line alone with every weight 1, the plain bend. `count(for:)` is how many wavelengths a bend that parts 400 and 700 nm by a number of pixels needs for neighbouring wavelengths to land about a pixel apart: 1 below a quarter pixel, otherwise that spread rounded up plus 2, at least 3 and at most a limit. `Picture.Glass.Body.tilts` is, for each colour, the weighted mean over a spectrum of 32 of (n(λ) − 1) / (index − 1): all 1 without dispersion, blue's above 1 and red's below with it.
- A `Picture.Glass.Pane` is a slab of a glass `thickness` thick over a `Picture.Plane.Region` of swift-geometry (a disc, a capsule, a rectangle with circular or continuous corners, `.superellipse`, a path, or a union of them), seen straight on. Its top stands at `height(at:)` a distance in from the region's boundary: `thickness` × (1 − (1 − u)^p)^(1/p) for u = inward / `rim` and p the `profile`, which meets the boundary upright and the level middle with no slope; from p = 3 on, the default, also with no bend, so lines seen through the rim run smoothly into the middle, where a quarter circle (p = 2) leaves a kink, and `thickness` from the rim on. `slope(at:)` is its derivative, 0 from the rim on and infinite on the boundary. `normal(at:)` is the top's unit normal in eye space, (s·gx, −s·gy, 1) at unit length for the region's gradient g and the slope s there, so it faces the eye where the top is level and leans out across the rim. With a profile of 2 and the rim and the thickness both the radius, a circular pane is a hemisphere: the flat glass and the solid body are one family.
- `Picture.Glass.Pane.Passage` is what light does at a point of a pane. With a body, light of an index is bent into the top by Snell's law, runs down to the flat bottom from the top's height, is bent out there, and runs on to the picture the pane's `distance` below; `offset(for:)` is how far aside that carries it, in picture units with y down, and `offset` is that at the glass's own index. A level top bends nothing, so in the middle every offset is zero and the picture is shown in place under any index and dispersion; an index of 1 bends nothing anywhere. Across the rim the top leans out, light is bent inward, and the picture is read further in the nearer the boundary, faster than the boundary comes: that is the lensing band, magnified and, nearest the edge, turned over. The tint is one coat, the same all over the pane: a pane over a flat colour shows that colour under its tint. Without a body every index reads at `offset(for:)` of the normal. `share` is Fresnel's `Refraction.Interface.reflectance(cosine:)` at the normal's z where the pane has `surroundings` and a body, and 0 without.
- A pane's `shadows` and a scene's `shadows` are drawn in order before its glass, and the glass shows the picture as it was before them. A `Picture.Glass.Shadow` is the glass's outline as drawn, a pane's region or the silhouette of all of a scene's glass, moved by `offset` with an edge of `softness`: the colour's alpha times `share(at:)` of the moved outline's signed distance, 1 / (1 + e^(1.7·d / softness)), the logistic curve that stands within 0.01 of a Gaussian-blurred edge; a thin dark one of no offset and a softness of half a pixel is the glass's dark edge. `with(alpha:)` is the shadow at another strength. Every part of a pane is a plain value, and the picture changes continuously with each, so a pane's region, thickness, rim, light and merge radius can change from frame to frame: two regions in a `union` that come within its radius join by a rounded neck, and part again, and the optics follow the joined shape.
- A glass's `surface` is `.skin(bend:)` or `.body(_:)`. A `Picture.Glass.Body` holds its `medium` (index 1.5 at the d line by default, and an Abbe number); the `distance` behind the glass's back at which the picture behind stands is the pane's or the scene material's (`.glass(_:distance:)`), and the optional `Surroundings` are the pane's or the scene's. A pane's back is its flat bottom; a body's is the plane across the line of sight through its farthest point from the eye, so the picture behind a body is one plane that light leaving any part of it travels on to. Light is bent by Snell's law where it goes in (`Refraction.Interface.refracted(_:at:)`) and again where it leaves the far side; where none can leave, the outcome is total internal reflection and it is mirrored back in. So a ball is a thick lens: a picture nearer behind it than its focus, about half its radius for an index of 1.5, is seen upright and enlarged (three times at no distance, more as it nears the focus), as a glass ball held over a page shows it, and a picture farther away is turned over, and both are squeezed toward the edges. `Picture.Glass.Body.enlargement(radius:distance:)` is how many times near its middle, for a ball of that radius and the picture that distance behind it, both in picture units: `Refraction.Ball.enlargement(at:)`, index / (2 − index) at no distance, negative past the focus, and 1 for an index of 1 or a radius that is not positive; a test holds the body renderer to within 10 % of it at three distances on a ball of 192 × 96 quads, the rest being the aberration of a read 2.5 pixels off the axis; and another to within 3 % at the same distances on balls of 48 × 24 and 24 × 12 seen along their poles, read one pixel across the axis: the vertex normals of swift-mesh are the sphere's own at every vertex, poles included, so a coarse ball's middle is out only by its flat faces. A body's thickness is the least side of the box around its mesh in its own space as view times model sizes it, which no turn of the camera changes; the tint is taken on along the way: for a tint of alpha a and a path l times that thickness its share is 1 - (1 - a)^l, so the thickest path through a ball takes the coat a pane's level middle takes. `Surroundings` are a colour `above` and one `below`, mixed by the height of a direction; the surface mirrors them by Fresnel's reflected share, and light that leaves further aside than `width` times the distance shows them in place of the picture. The pixel law puts these together, and the light is added last.
- Text is set by swift-typesetting, whose `Typesetting.Text` sets a string in a font as glyph outlines at places in a plane with y up; this package only draws it, and a renderer never sees a font. `Picture.Plane.Path(_:)` of a set path is that path in picture space, y negated and x as it is, so a glyph's `path` lands at its place below and right of the first baseline's left end, and its `outline` stands upright around its own origin. Negation is exact, so a text draws the same pixels it drew when this package set it.
- `Typesetting.Text.shape(_:)` is the text's `path` in picture space with a fill. `placed(_:at:anchor:level:)` is that shape in a group moved so that the text's `point(at:level:)`, the first line's `level` (its baseline by default) at the left end, the middle or the right end of the measure, lands on the point: by the point less the anchor's x along x and by the point plus the level's height along y. So at `.cap(0.5)` the capitals' top and bottom are equally far from the point. `centered(_:at:anchor:top:)` is a block centred on the point: the point lies midway between the first line's `top`, the capitals' top by default, and the last line's baseline; for one line it is `placed` at `.cap(0.5)`. `bounds(at:anchor:level:)` is the box of the ink `placed` puts down: the bounding box of the path under the same move, nil for a text that draws nothing.
- `Typesetting.Text.wrapped(radius:start:anchor:level:)` stands the glyphs on a band in space: for each glyph a model matrix that takes its outline in picture space, `Picture.Plane.Path(glyph.outline)`, as the plane z = 0 around its own origin, to its place on a circle of the radius about the y axis through the first line's level, upright with y up and facing out, the middle of its advance at its arc length from the angle `start` about y, where 0 is +z and the text reads toward +x. `Picture.Scene.projected(_:in:tolerance:)` draws such a flat path through the scene's camera: the path flattened to within the tolerance, in the model's units, and each vertex put at `position(of:through:)` of (x, y, 0); a perspective keeps straight lines straight, so this is exact up to the flattening, and nil when a vertex has no place. Depth is not kept, so a caller orders the glyphs from far to near.
- `Picture.Renderer` is a `@Witness` struct with one closure, `render`, which takes the picture borrowed and the raster `inout` and fails with `Picture.Renderer.Error`. The macro's labelled method `render(picture:raster:)` and the `render(_:into:)` that makes it a `Renderer.Protocol` of swift-renderer stand side by side and both call the closure once.
- `Picture.Renderer` is its own `Witness.Key`, and the conformance lives in `Picture CPU`, the one target that sees a renderer: its live value is `Picture.Renderer.cpu`, and preview and test follow live. Importing `Picture` alone gives the type, not the key. `Picture.Renderer.absent` draws nothing and fails with `.absent`. `Picture.Renderer.Error` is `Hashable`, and so its every payload is: a `Picture.Image.Failure` is equal to another exactly when their errors are of one type and equal, and hashes as its error does, which is why it takes an error that is `Hashable` rather than any error, and holds the error rather than its description, so the caller can still switch on the reason; it describes itself as its error does. A renderer set with `withWitnesses` is the one `Witness.Context` gives inside that scope, and only there. `unimplemented()` fails with `.unimplemented`, carrying the witness's own error.
- A renderer draws onto the raster it is given, over what is already there, and changes nothing when it fails before drawing. The same renderer on the same machine gives equal rasters for equal pictures.
- `W3C_PNG.Image.init(_:)` of `Picture PNG Integration` makes an RGBA image of 8 bits a channel from a raster of working pixels, marked `sRGB` with the perceptual intent and the sRGB gamma, the raster's rows top first. Each pixel's colour is divided by its alpha, held to 0…1, put through `IEC_61966.SRGB.TransferFunction.encoded(_:)`, multiplied by 255 and rounded to the nearest whole number, halves up. Alpha is held to 0…1, multiplied by 255 and rounded the same way, without the transfer function. What is not a number counts as 0, and a pixel with no alpha is transparent black.
- Each of the 256 sRGB levels written as an opaque `Picture.Color` comes back as that level, and so does each of the 255 alphas above zero written with white. A raster with no pixels fails with `W3C_PNG.Image.Error.dimensions`, as PNG has no empty image.
- `Raster<Picture.Pixel>.init(_:)` of `Picture PNG Integration` is the way back: it reads a `W3C_PNG.Image` of every colour type at every depth swift-w3c-png reads (greyscale at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 bits, greyscale with alpha, truecolour and truecolour with alpha at 8 and 16, indexed at 1, 2, 4 and 8), with the image's width and height and its rows top first. A colour sample s of a depth whose largest sample is m becomes `IEC_61966.SRGB.TransferFunction.linear(s / m)` worked in double precision and rounded to `Float`, so a 16-bit sample keeps its 16 bits and an 8-bit one is the sRGB level table's value; a palette entry is read as its three 8-bit levels. Alpha is s / m, rounded to `Float`, without the transfer function; an image without an alpha channel is opaque, and the colour is multiplied by alpha. swift-w3c-png reads no `tRNS` chunk, so transparency comes only from an alpha channel. The samples are taken as sRGB unless the image says otherwise: a `cICP` other than `W3C_PNG.CICP.sRGB` (primaries 1, transfer 13, full range), or, with neither `cICP` nor `sRGB`, a `gAMA` other than `W3C_PNG.Gamma.sRGB`, fails with `Picture.PNG.Error.encoding`; an `sRGB` chunk wins over `gAMA`, and an image with none of the three is sRGB. A palette index with no entry fails with `Picture.PNG.Error.index`.
- A raster whose pixels 8 bits hold exactly, each an sRGB level's linear light per colour and a level over 255 as alpha with the colour multiplied by it, is itself after it is written as a `W3C_PNG.Image` and read back, also through `W3C_PNG.serialize(_:filter:level:)` and `W3C_PNG.parse(_:)`; a test holds this for every level as colour and as alpha, half-transparent and transparent pixels among them.
- `Picture.Renderer.cpu` of `Picture CPU` draws, with the `Threads` trait, on as many threads as the machine has logical processors, and `cpu(threads:)` on at most that many (none counts as one), into a copy of the raster's pixels; it puts the copy in place only when the whole picture is drawn, so a failure anywhere, however deep in a layer, leaves the raster exactly as it was. Before it draws a picture it gives every image that names its picture its pixels: the distinct sources of the whole picture are gathered, each is taken from the renderer's cache or drawn through the renderer itself, the ones to draw side by side on the threads, in rounds where a source waits for every source that its own picture names, however deep, so that a source that another names is drawn once and first, and the result is the picture drawn with those images made beforehand by `drawn(through:)`, bit for bit; a test holds this. The cache belongs to the renderer value and its copies, so one value drawn through for every frame keeps a part that does not change across all of them, also when frames are drawn on several threads at once, and `Picture.Renderer.cpu`, which makes a new value each time it is read, keeps nothing between two readings. It holds at most 2²⁵ pixels, or what `cpu(threads:keeping:)` is given, 16 bytes each; past that the rasters asked for longest ago go first, and with no pixels to keep every picture draws its images afresh. Sources are found by their value: by the hash made when each was made, and then by comparing drawings. Each pass that touches many pixels (a fill, a layer's composite, an effect, each axis of a blur, sprite and scene tiles) is cut into parts that do not overlap: bands of whole rows, strips of whole columns for the vertical axis of a blur, or tiles. Threads take the parts in any order, and a pixel is computed by the same arithmetic whichever thread takes it, so every thread count gives the same raster bit for bit; a test holds this for one, four and nine threads. Nothing else depends on time, threads or addresses, so the same picture on the same machine gives the same raster; bit-equal rasters across machines are not promised. Threads are started with `Kernel.Thread.spawn` of swift-kernel for each such pass and joined before it ends; a part is about 16 384 pixels, and a smaller pass stays on the calling thread. The threads come with the `Threads` trait, which a consumer asks for as it asks for `PNG` (`traits: ["PNG", "Threads"]`); without it swift-kernel is not built, `cpu` is one thread, and every part runs on the calling thread, with the same raster.
- `Picture.Store` of `Picture Store` keeps bytes made of drawn pictures in a directory, one file each, named by a `Picture.Store.Key`: the SHA-256 of the store's format (4: an earlier format's entries are simply missed), its edition, the form the caller names for what it made (a PNG, a frame's 8-bit pixels), the raster's width and height, the memory of the one colour it held before drawing, and the picture's `Swift.Mirror.bytes(of:into:)` from swift-standard-library-extensions, fed to the digest as they are written. Those bytes are a function of the picture's value alone, so equal pictures have one key whether their arrays share storage or are copies; a picture writes its elements and a path its subpaths and segments through the writer's `shared(_:_:)`, so a shared array is paid for once. A picture those bytes cannot show whole has no key and is never kept. `keep(_:for:)` writes the entry whole under a temporary name and renames it into place without syncing the disk, so processes may keep entries in one directory at once; `bytes(for:)` gives the bytes back as kept, or nil for an entry that is missing, cut short, damaged or of another key, which its length and Adler-32 tell.
- The store cannot tell renderers apart: its edition must name the renderer and the edition of its drawing. `Picture.Store.cpu(_:)` uses the edition of `Picture CPU`, a number raised with every change that can change a pixel it draws. Its guard is a test in `Picture CPU Tests` that draws one small picture for each of fills, round and mitred strokes, a linear and a radial gradient, an image placed affinely, one wrapped and read at the nearest pixel and one in perspective, a clip, layers that blend and add, every effect, a glow, text, soft and hard sprites, a scene with a shaded model, a wireframe, points and haze, a glass skin, a glass body with light and one through a perspective lens, frost, shadows, surroundings and adaptation, and glass panes, one inside a layer, on one thread and on four, and compares a digest of each raster with the digests recorded beside the edition; when one changes it fails with "drawing changed: raise the edition and record the new digests". A change to a feature none of those pictures reaches passes it unseen. What the key does not cover is outside the guarantee: a renderer changed without a new edition, and a picture holding a pointer, which is keyed by its address. A `Picture.Image.Source` keeps a `Hasher` value that differs from process to process and is written as its `Swift.Mirror.Canonical` value instead, its drawing (a part's picture, rectangle, scale and samples); a test fails when the source gains a stored property, until the canonical value holds it. A picture, an element, a shape, a paint, an image and a gradient write themselves as `Swift.Mirror.Written`, a path's subpaths and line segments with them, since reflection at about a microsecond a part would make a picture of 90,000 paths cost seconds to key; a test lists each one's stored properties by `Mirror` and fails when one is added, and each `switch` over cases is exhaustive. Everything else in a picture is written by reflection. The store is for iteration: a drawing of record runs without it.
- It covers a path with `Raster.coverage(of:rule:in:tolerance:)` of swift-raster, the exact area of the path flattened to within 0.1 pixel of the raster, over the pixels its bounding box reaches. A stroke's outline is made in the shape's own space, so a group that scales scales the pen, at 0.1 pixel divided by the root of the sum of the squares of the transform's linear part, and filled under `nonzero`. A paint is read once per covered pixel, at the pixel's centre taken back into the shape's space. A transform that cannot be inverted draws nothing, though a stroke the geometry refuses still fails.
- A layer of opacity 0 under an operator that keeps the backdrop where its sheet is transparent is not drawn at all, as it would give the backdrop exactly; a test holds this with a content this renderer refuses to draw. A layer's sheet covers only the pixels its composite reads, cut to the box outside which its content draws nothing. An operator that changes the backdrop where the sheet is transparent (`clear`, `copy`, `sourceIn`, `destinationIn`, `sourceOut`, `destinationAtop`) changes every pixel of the canvas inside the clip; the other seven leave such pixels exactly as they are and are visited only where the sheet holds something.
- Effects are applied in raster space. Every effect but `blur` is its point map conjugated by the transform from the layer's space, so it is exact under any invertible transform. The sheet a layer draws its content on is worked out backwards from the pixels its composite reads: a blur reads its kernel's radius further on each side, `displacement` its amplitude times √2 times the root of the sum of the squares of the transform's linear part, every other effect the box around the points its `source(of:)` names for the pixel centres; each plus one pixel for bilinear reading, and each sheet is cut to the box outside which it is known to be transparent. So the raster's edge never shows, and the same picture in a larger raster gives the same pixels up to rounding. Between pixel centres a sheet is read bilinearly and is transparent past its box. A sheet larger than 2²⁶ pixels is refused with `.unsupported`.
- A blur takes the deviation along each axis in pixels. Below 2 pixels that axis is `Raster.convolved` with `Raster.Kernel.gaussian(deviation:)` of swift-raster, cut off at three deviations rounded up and normalised, with a transparent edge. From 2 pixels on it is `Raster.smoothed` with `Raster.Cascade.gaussian(deviation:count:)` of five passes, whose variance is the Gaussian's exactly and whose cost does not grow with the deviation: each such axis differs from the convolution by at most 0.032 times the brightest channel within reach, so a blur by at most 0.064 (swift-raster states and tests the bound). Its reach is the cascade's, about 3.9 deviations. Under a transform with linear part A it is the Gaussian whose covariance is σ²·A·Aᵀ, which this renderer draws only when that is diagonal, that is when the two rows of A are perpendicular (translations, rotations with a uniform scale, and any of these followed by a scale along the axes): each axis then takes σ times the length of its row of A. Any other blur, such as one under a shear, fails with `.unsupported`. `zoom` reads the sheet along the line toward the centre at scales s spread evenly over [1 − amount, 1]. When 1 − amount is positive it is drawn in two passes: with r = (1 − amount)^(1/8), a fine zoom of amount 1 − r (the mean of the sheet at the midpoints of as many equal steps of its stretch as the stretch is long in pixels, at least one and at most 4096, summed in `Double` and divided by the count), then at each point the mean of that result read bilinearly at the scales r^j for j from 0 to 7, weighted by r^j, the width of the piece of the range each read covers; so a stretch of L pixels costs about L / 8 + 8 reads instead of L. On a field of sharp radial rings under a zoom of 0.6 it lies within 0.02 of the mean over 2000 evenly spread fractions in every channel (one pass with a read per pixel of stretch: 0.016), and a test holds the 0.02; an amount of 1 or more is drawn in the one pass; an amount of 0, a pixelation cell with no area and a kaleidoscope with no segments are skipped. A kaleidoscope reads the sheet at `source(of:)` of each pixel centre bilinearly, but where taps of weight of that read lie across a mirror from the first wedge (`holds(_:)` false at their centres) it leaves them out and shares their weight among the rest, so nothing from across a mirror bleeds into its edge and the edge is as sharp as the sheet; with spokes or a seam, each pixel then becomes `pixel(_:at:footprint:spokes:seam:)` at its centre taken back into the layer's space, the footprint being one pixel's side there, one over the root of the transform's determinant.
- Sprites are read at pixel centres: each pixel takes `Picture.Sprites.light(at:)` at its centre, a sprite's distance taken only where its square is below the radius's, taken back into the sprites' space, summed over only the sprites whose square reaches the pixel's tile of 32 × 32, in their own order. A sprite that does not reach a point adds nothing there, so this is the full sum bit for bit. The light goes on with `lighter`. Sprites smaller than a pixel are not supersampled.
- A scene is sampled at 4 × 4 points in each pixel and each pixel takes their mean, their sum times one over their count, or the sample itself where all are one (so is every mean of samples this renderer takes); each point shows the nearest surface there over what was behind. Faces are fanned into triangles and cut at the near plane in clip space, a new corner being the model point at the same fraction; a fragment beyond the far plane is not drawn. A wireframe edge is a quadrilateral `width` wide around the projected edge, and a point a polygon of at least 8 sides around the projected vertex, each at its vertices' depth, so faces, edges and points of one scene hide each other by depth; of equal depths the triangle listed first wins. A shaded triangle's sample point takes the perspective-correct mix of its vertex normals (`normals(per: .vertex)` of swift-mesh) through `Camera.direction(_:through:)`, and the shade's pixel there, its ramp taken out of sRGB once. Triangles are sampled with `Raster.fragments(of:in:empty:_:)` of swift-raster, tile by tile, each tile given the triangles whose box reaches it in their own order.
- A scene's haze is applied to each sample point: the depth that won there is taken back to a distance as `Camera.distance(at:)` does, with the lens's coefficients taken once, and the surface's pixel becomes `Haze.pixel(_:at:)` of it before it goes over what was behind.
- Glass is a second pass. Where its nearest surface is nearer than every other model's at a point, the point takes the glass's pixel over the picture as it stood after those other models, read bilinearly at the point plus `offset(for:)` moved by the linear part of the group's transform. The normal is the perspective-correct mix of the mesh's vertex normals (`normals(per: .vertex)` of swift-mesh) put through `Camera.direction(_:in:)`. Past the canvas the picture is read at its nearest edge pixel, so glass near the raster's edge can depend on the raster's size, against the law that a picture does not. A translucent face shows the picture behind the scene, not farther faces of its own scene.
- Glass with a body is drawn from the same nearest surface. Its far side is the nearest surface facing away from the eye, held as depth and normal at 2 × 2 points to a pixel over the scene's box. From the point's place in eye space the line of sight is bent in; the path's length starts as the thickness along the line of sight and is corrected three times to end at the far side's depth where its end is drawn; the far side's normal there bends the light out. Each colour is then read where its light meets the plane of the picture behind, `distance` picture units behind the model's farthest vertex, taken into eye space at the scale the lens draws there, and drawn relative to the point so that light going straight on reads at the point itself. Green finds the way and red and blue follow paths of the same length. This is not ray tracing: the far side is looked up in the picture, so a body hidden behind another part of itself is entered once, light is not followed through a second body, and a path that ends where no far side is drawn goes straight on.
- A body's light of the glass's own index finds the way and light of every other index follows a path of the same length under its own; a pane's light of each index takes its own way. Where the 400 and 700 nm ends of the spectrum read less than a quarter pixel apart the point is read once, at the glass's own index, so without dispersion, and wherever the glass bends nothing, a point costs one read; elsewhere it is read at `Spectrum.count(for:)` wavelengths (at most 32) and gathered, so the extra reads fall only where the colours part, in a rim, at a neck and at a body's silhouette. The frosted picture is drawn once per glass over the box it can read: by `Raster.smoothed` with a five-pass cascade, and from a deviation of 6 pixels on at a whole factor of a third of the deviation, at most 8, coarser and read back bilinearly, which a blur that wide cannot tell from the full raster; past its box and past the canvas it reads the nearest pixel kept.
- A body's outline is found from what is drawn: at 2 × 2 points to a pixel, whether glass is nearer than every opaque model there, and from that the signed distance to its silhouette by a two-pass sweep that carries each point's nearest point across it (Danielsson's vector distance), read bilinearly. The line along the outline and the glass's shadows are drawn from that distance as a pane's are from its region's, the shadows after the opaque models and before the glass, and the glass reads the picture as it stood before them; a scene's shadows fall from the outline of all its glass. Where the line of sight grazes a body, within a cosine of 0.2, its reads are drawn back toward the point itself with no slope at either end, all the way at a cosine of 0, since a few pixels of silhouette would otherwise read far and wide and show moiré; the line covers them. A pixel all of whose 4 × 4 points fall on glass takes the mean of the central 2 × 2, centred on the pixel's centre, and an edge pixel all of them; the picture behind a scene's glass is frosted over the scene's box grown by half its larger side, and read past that at the nearest pixel kept.
- A pane is drawn in raster space. Its region's signed distance is kept at every eighth pixel centre of its box, and as a signed distance changes no faster than the point moves, a pixel known from there to lie outside is left alone, one known to lie in the level middle is the glass over the frosted picture read in place, and only the rest samples the region: a pixel within 1.5 pixels of the boundary or under the light's line is the mean of 3 × 3 points, their sum times one over their count, one in the outer two fifths of the rim of 2 × 2, and the rest one point, a point outside the region keeping what was there. Each band of rows samples its own copy of the region, so threads never share its storage. The distances assume a transform that scales evenly; under one that does not the pixels near the boundary are drawn as if it did.
- `swift test -c release --enable-all-traits --filter Timing` measures full-HD frames of every kind this renderer draws, on one thread and on all, and prints each; its output, not this file, is the record of speed.
